\ourchapter{RoPART: design and implementation}\label{ch:ropart}

Two operators will be used throughout this chapter and are worth defining here. The planar rotation matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rotmat}
  R(\varphi) \;=\;
  \begin{bmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \phantom{-}\cos\varphi \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

and the wrap-to$(-\pi,\pi]$ operator, which makes any angular quantity well defined
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wrap}
  \operatorname{wrap}(\alpha) \;=\; \bigl((\alpha + \pi) \bmod 2\pi\bigr) - \pi
  \;\in\; (-\pi, \pi].
\end{equation}

\section{The \texorpdfstring{\SEtwo}{SE(2)} target}\label{sec:se2-target}
The per-pair target in PART was defined as \dxy{} and the per-pair target of RoPART can be thought of as \dxy{} plus \dphi{}. In other words, PART supervises the translation subgroup and RoPART closes the group by adding rotation as the missing generator.
We can think of the patch state as a group element - sampling gives patch $i$ a centre $c_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and an orientation $\varphi_i$, i.e. an element of \SEtwo{}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:se2-element}
  g_i \;=\; (c_i, \varphi_i) \;\cong\;
  \begin{bmatrix} R(\varphi_i) & c_i \\ 0^{\!\top} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in \SEtwo .
\end{equation}
Going back to PART, the patches there were axis aligned ($\varphi \equiv 0$) and $g_i$ lives in the translation subgroup $\mathbb{R}^2 \triangleleft \SEtwo$.
While group-theoretically relative transform between the ordered pair is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:se2-relative}
  g_i^{-1} g_j \;=\; \bigl(\, R(-\varphi_i)(c_j - c_i),\; \varphi_j - \varphi_i \,\bigr).
\end{equation}
it is not what RoPART regresses. The actual implemented target for the ordered pair $(i,j)$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target-ropart}
  \theta_{ij}
  \;=\;
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \Delta x \\ \Delta y \\ \cos\Delta\varphi \\ \sin\Delta\varphi
  \end{bmatrix}_{ij}
  \;=\;
  \begin{bmatrix}
    (c^{x}_{j} - c^{x}_{i}) / P \\[2pt]
    (c^{y}_{j} - c^{y}_{i}) / P \\[2pt]
    \cos(\varphi_j - \varphi_i) \\[2pt]
    \sin(\varphi_j - \varphi_i)
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \Delta\varphi \;\triangleq\; \varphi_j - \varphi_i .
\end{equation}
Note that this target is not exactly \SEtwo, but rather \SEtwo with the translation read in a global frame, not $g_i^{-1}g_j$ exactly, as the translation channels are differenced in the global image frame. In other words, the two frames coincide up to the $R(-\varphi_i)$ factor. This decision was made for better compatibility and comparison with the PART method. This enabled adding rotation on top of translation similarly to how $\Delta w$ and $\Delta h$ were added. Note that $\dx$ and $\dy$ contain $P$ in the denominator as every patch is of the same size. Another point worth mentioning is that global rotation ($\varphi_i$) is never supervised, only the differences are. 

When the targets are constructed, they must first pass the structural invariants checks to confirm that they have been generated correctly. These are:
\begin{align}
  \theta^{xy}_{ii} &= 0, &
  (\cos\Delta\varphi_{ii}, \sin\Delta\varphi_{ii}) &= (1, 0),
  \label{eq:inv-identity}\\
  \theta^{xy}_{ij} &= -\,\theta^{xy}_{ji}, &
  \cos\Delta\varphi_{ij} &= \phantom{-}\cos\Delta\varphi_{ji},
  \quad
  \sin\Delta\varphi_{ij} = -\sin\Delta\varphi_{ji},
  \label{eq:inv-negsym}\\
  \theta^{xy}_{ik} &= \theta^{xy}_{ij} + \theta^{xy}_{jk}, &
  \Delta\varphi_{ik} &= \operatorname{wrap}(\Delta\varphi_{ij} + \Delta\varphi_{jk}).
  \label{eq:inv-composition}
\end{align}
In words, rotation relative to self should be zero (identity), rotation from i to j should be equal to rotation from j to i (negative symmetry), and rotation from i to k should be equal to the sum of rotations from i to j and from j to k (composition). Note that these equations play double role - once when targets are constructed and once when predictions are evaluated. The second role ensures that the model took no shortcuts, e.g. by reading a blur signature. Out of all the checks, composition is the strongest and symmetry may hold when it does not. 

For a batch of $b$ images, then, the builder emits every ordered pair at once,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target-table}
  T \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times C \times N \times N},
  \qquad T[\,\cdot\,, :, i, j] = \theta_{ij},
\end{equation}
with the build order fixing $C$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:num-channels}
  C \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    2, & (\Delta x, \Delta y) \quad \text{- translation-only baseline},\\
    4, & +\,(\cos\Delta\varphi, \sin\Delta\varphi) \quad \text{- RoPART}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Note that $C$ is also the output width of the relative head which makes the build order a one-line change. This allows proper attribution of the supervision effect separate from the effect of rotation working as data augmentation.

\section{Angle representation and loss}\label{sec:angle-representation}
To represent rotation, a simple direct angle regression may be used. This is a poor neural target: an angle is a point on the unit circle, so any scalar parametrisation is discontinuous at the $\pm\pi$ wrap-around, where two near-identical orientations sit at opposite ends of the range. See this example:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wrap-discontinuity}
  \bigl(\,(\pi - \varepsilon) - (-\pi + \varepsilon)\,\bigr)^2 \;\longrightarrow\; 4\pi^2,
  \qquad\text{while the true angular separation is } 2\varepsilon \to 0 .
\end{equation}
The network must approximate a discontinuous function, which in the case of two orientations separated by $2\varepsilon$ either side of the seam incur a squared error of $\approx 4\pi^2$. Zhou et al.~\cite{rotcont} show such discontinuous representations are harder to learn and they prescribe a continuous embedding of the form $(\cos\Delta\theta, \sin\Delta\theta)$. Encoding the angle on the unit circle as $(\cos\Delta\varphi,\sin\Delta\varphi)$ removes the seam and is therefore a better target as it is a function that is continuous in $\varphi$. 

Formally, the target angle is mapped to a point on the unit circle, $S^1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:circle-encoding}
  \Delta\varphi \;\longmapsto\; (\cos\Delta\varphi,\, \sin\Delta\varphi) \in S^1,
\end{equation}
and the head regresses that point as two \emph{unconstrained} scalars $(\hat c, \hat s)$ and recovers the angle via
\begin{equation}\label{eq:atan2}
  \widehat{\Delta\varphi} \;=\; \operatorname{atan2}(\hat s, \hat c).
\end{equation}

Note that MSE on the circle is a cosine loss and if the prediction lies on the unit circle, the two losses coincide up to a factor of two:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mse-equals-cosine}
  (\hat c - \cos\Delta\varphi)^2 + (\hat s - \sin\Delta\varphi)^2
  \;=\; \lVert \hat u \rVert^2 + 1 - 2\,\hat u^{\!\top} u
  \;\overset{\lVert\hat u\rVert = 1}{=}\; 2\bigl(1 - \cos(\widehat{\Delta\varphi} - \Delta\varphi)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\hat u = (\hat c, \hat s)$ and $u = (\cos\Delta\varphi, \sin\Delta\varphi)$. Note the caveat: the head output is not norm constrained, so \cref{eq:mse-equals-cosine} only holds up to the $\lVert\hat u\rVert^2$ term (this is because plain MSE additionally penalises radial deviation - a free confidence channel). The runs used MSE, not the cosine form.

Predicting several relative quantities from a shared backbone is a multi-task regression. This means that channels with different units and convergence rate may interfere and a single fixed loss weighting may prove hard to tune and may not be optimal throughout training. Indeed, in PART~\cite{part} itself adding the relative scale and aspect ratio terms delayed the convergence of translation terms relative to the baseline. Furthermore, PART ~\cite{part} used a loss function that applied plain squared error to every channel through equal weights - while this could work with just translation, it greatly complicates learning rotation and the loss was designed with this issue in mind. One established solution is to make the weighting adaptive. Kendall et al.~\cite{kendall} propose weighting each task by a learned homoscedastic uncertainty which automatically downweights noisier tasks. RoPART does not learn the weighting; it uses fixed, data-driven analogue that divides by an observed target variance. 

To introduce the loss, first define the four channels of $\theta_{ij}$ (\cref{eq:target-ropart}) split into two groups by what they measure - the translation rows and the rotation rows:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:channel-groups}
    \mathcal{G}_{xy} = \{\Delta x,\, \Delta y\},
    \qquad
    \mathcal{G}_{\varphi} = \{\cos\Delta\varphi,\, \sin\Delta\varphi\},
  \end{equation}
  i.e.\ rows $1$-$2$ and rows $3$-$4$ of \cref{eq:target-ropart}
  (\py{[:, 0:2]} and \py{[:, 2:4]} in \py{losses.RelativeMSE}, which is $0$-indexed). The loss is grouped MSE over $M$ sampled pairs, with the two channel groups:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss-none}
  \mathcal{L}
  \;=\; w_{xy}\,\frac{1}{2 b M}\!\!\sum_{k \in \mathcal{G}_{xy}}\sum_{n,m}\!\bigl(\hat\theta_{knm} - \theta_{knm}\bigr)^2
  \;+\; w_{\varphi}\,\frac{1}{2 b M}\!\!\sum_{k \in \mathcal{G}_{\varphi}}\sum_{n,m}\!\bigl(\hat\theta_{knm} - \theta_{knm}\bigr)^2 .
\end{equation}
where $k$ indexes channels within a group, $n=1,\dots,b$ indexes images in the batch, and $m=1\dots,M$ indexes the sampled ordered pairs of \cref{eq:pair-sampling}. Note that the loss is taken over the $M$ sampled pairs, not the full $N\times N$ table. Note that if we set $w_\varphi=0$ and drop $\mathcal{G}\varphi$ we recover PART's loss.

There is an issue with the form above when $w_{xy}=w_\varphi=1$ the translation term is $\mathrm{Var}(\Delta x)\approx 6$ whereas the rotation group is bounded by $1-(\sin a/a)^4$ \cref {eq:rot-floor-bounded}, which at $a=30^\circ$ is $0.169$ - a $\sim$30$\times$ imbalance. In this case, $\Delta\varphi$ collapses to a mean-predictor. 

To solve this issue we use variance balancing, where each channel's squared error is divided by that channel's own detached batch target variance, i.e. its own mean-prediction floor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss-variance}
  \mathcal{L}
  \;=\;
  \sum_{G \in \{\mathcal{G}_{xy}, \mathcal{G}_{\varphi}\}} \!\! w_G \,
  \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{k \in G}
  \frac{\operatorname{MSE}_k}{\max\!\bigl(\operatorname{sg}[\operatorname{Var}(\theta_k)],\, \epsilon\bigr)},
  \qquad \epsilon = 10^{-3},
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ is the stop-gradient and both $\operatorname{MSE}_k$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(\theta_k)$ are taken over the pooled $(b \times M)$ batch sample
(biased variance). Every term is then a fraction of its own floor, so both
groups are $O(1)$ irrespective of units or rotation range, and $w_{xy}, w_\varphi$
become actual relative-importance knobs. This is the form used in every reported run. Note the $\epsilon$ term as an argument in the $\max$ function - this is to prevent amplification which created NaNs during training when small range of rotations was tested. The clamp did not 'activate' when the rotation bounds were greater than $30^\circ$. The actual weights used were $w_{xy}=1$, $w_\varphi=0.1$.


\section{Patch position convention}\label{sec:position-convention}
For an axis-aligned patch (box) $[x_s,y_s,x_e,y_e]$ with $x_e=x_s+P,y_e=y_s+P$ there are two choices of an anchor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:anchors}
  c_i^{\text{centre}} = \Bigl(\tfrac{x_s + x_e}{2},\, \tfrac{y_s + y_e}{2}\Bigr)
  \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad
  c_i^{\text{corner}} = (x_s, y_s).
\end{equation}
With no rotation, i.e. $\varphi=0$ there is no cost associated with choosing one option over the other. This can be seen in the equation below:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:anchor-equivalence}
  c^{\text{centre}}_j - c^{\text{centre}}_i
  \;=\; \bigl(c^{\text{corner}}_j + \tfrac{P}{2}\mathbf{1}\bigr) - \bigl(c^{\text{corner}}_i + \tfrac{P}{2}\mathbf{1}\bigr)
  \;=\; c^{\text{corner}}_j - c^{\text{corner}}_i .
\end{equation}
So the two anchors differ by the same fixed offset for every patch. However, once patches carry $\varphi$ the corner is no longer a fixed offset from the centre - it moves with the patch:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner-entangles}
  c^{\text{corner}}_i(\varphi_i) \;=\; c^{\text{centre}}_i - R(\varphi_i)\,\tfrac{P}{2}\mathbf{1},
\end{equation}
So by choosing to anchor at the corners, \dxy would carry a $\varphi$-dependent term - the translation channel would lose its control properties for the rotation channel. The centre is the unique fixed point of $R(\varphi_i)$.%
\footnote{A half-pixel detail that does not affect the targets: the target builder uses the
geometric centre $(x_s + x_e)/2$, while the rotated pixel read uses the pixel-index centre
$(x_s + x_e - 1)/2$, so that an integer coordinate lands on a pixel centre. The two differ by
the same half pixel for every patch, so they cancel in the difference exactly as in
\cref{eq:anchor-equivalence}.}

\section{Rotating pixels}\label{sec:rotating-pixels}
To create the rotated patches fed into the model, they have to be constructed via a pipeline. First of all, a $P\times P$ square rotated by an arbitrary angle is not contained in its own axis-aligned footprint - it is contained in the diagonal. We need to sample with a margin to avoid empty corners. Read from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rot-window}
  W \;=\; \bigl\lceil P\sqrt{2} \,\bigr\rceil,
  \qquad
  m \;=\; \Bigl\lfloor \tfrac{W - P + 1}{2} \Bigr\rfloor ,
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the edge margin. At $P=32$: $W=46,m=7$. Note that this narrows the range and changes the translation floor - note that this introduces changes in the target distribution between baseline and rotation arms. With the margin set, we independently and uniformly sample top-left corners over the valid integer offsets:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:box-sampling}
  x_s, y_s \;\sim\; \mathcal{U}\{\,m,\, m+1,\, \dots,\, S - P - m\,\},
  \qquad
  M_{\text{pos}} \;=\; S - P - 2m + 1 ,
\end{equation}
Positions are continuous in the off-grid sense and patches may overlap.

With the box sampled, we can now sample one orientation per patch, i.i.d:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:angle-sampling}
  \varphi_i \;\sim\;
  \begin{cases}
    \mathcal{U}[0, 2\pi) & \text{full circle},\\
    \mathcal{U}[-a, +a] & \text{bounded, } a \in \{30^\circ, 60^\circ, 90^\circ\},\\
    \mathcal{U}\{0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\} & \text{quad}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Note that $a$ defines rotation bounds and quad is a special rotation model where only multiples of $90^\circ$ are selected (similar to RotNet~\cite{rotnet}).

To actually extract the pixels, we could naively grab the diagonal window, rotate, centre-crop and resize. Instead, perform a single inverse warp: for output pixel at patch-local, centred coordinates $(l_x,l_y)$ with $l \in \{-\tfrac{P-1}{2}, \dots, \tfrac{P-1}{2}\}$, the source coordinate is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-warp}
  \begin{bmatrix} s_x \\ s_y \end{bmatrix}
  \;=\;
  \begin{bmatrix} c^{x}_i \\ c^{y}_i \end{bmatrix}
  + R(\varphi_i)
  \begin{bmatrix} l_x \\ l_y \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
and the patch value is the bilinear sample $p(l_x, l_y) = I(s_x, s_y)$. Note that the naive approach contains two interpolating steps (rotate and resize) which would result in a compounded blur. Additionally, the blur is as small as it can be and is exactly bilinear - this will allow us to reason about it. Bilinear interpolation is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bilinear}
  I(s_x, s_y) \;=\; \sum_{u,v \in \{0,1\}}
    \bigl(1 - |u - \delta_x|\bigr)\bigl(1 - |v - \delta_y|\bigr)\;
    I\bigl[\lfloor s_x \rfloor + u,\; \lfloor s_y \rfloor + v\bigr].
\end{equation}
Where $\delta = s - \lfloor s \rfloor$ as the fractional part. Note that the kernel is separable and its support depends on $(\delta_x,\delta_y)$ and $(\delta_x,\delta_y)$ is a function of $\varphi_i$ - this is exactly the confound.

Side note for the quad path: it is a permutation, not a resampling.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quad-k}
  k \;=\; \Bigl(\operatorname{round}\!\bigl(\varphi_i \big/ \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\Bigr) \bmod 4,
  \qquad
  p \;=\; \operatorname{rot90}^{k}\bigl(\text{crop}_{P\times P}\bigr),
\end{equation}
In this case $\delta_x=\delta_y=0$ and \cref{eq:bilinear} generates a copy - no angle-correlated blur exists. Thus quad arm is confound-free by construction.

With patches available, now the next step is to pack them into a pseudo-image. Patch $k$ is placed row-major in a $\sqrt{N}\times\sqrt{N}$ tiling, so the ViT's stride-$P$ convolution recovers the tokens one-to-one:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:retile}
  \hat I\bigl[\,\cdot\,,\; r P + u,\; c P + v\,\bigr] \;=\; p_k[\,\cdot\,, u, v],
  \qquad r = \bigl\lfloor k / \sqrt{N} \bigr\rfloor,\; c = k \bmod \sqrt{N}.
\end{equation}
It is extremely important to emphasise that $k$ is merely the sampling index, completely unrelated to where the patch came from. It is in no way a position embedding. To the encoder, the pseudo-image is an unordered set.

Finally, the standard channel normalisation is applied after packing:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalise}
  \tilde I \;=\; (\hat I - \mu)/\sigma,
  \quad \mu = (0.485, 0.456, 0.406),
  \quad \sigma = (0.229, 0.224, 0.225).
\end{equation}
This is the only augmentation used in the pretraining. Upstream work applied full DeiT augmentation. In order to reduce the number of possible confounds, we opted not to follow suit.

\section{Interpolation controls by construction}\label{sec:interpolation-controls}
With rotation, resampling is unavoidable and loses detail as a function of $\varphi$. It is also possible that a model can obtain a high score on the pretext task by simply reading the blur. Therefore we need to measure the blur to decide how to mitigate the issue. One measurement for magnitude we can use is mean absolute pixel gradient - a high-frequency-energy proxy:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sharpness}
  E(\varphi) \;=\; \bigl\langle\, |\partial_x p_\varphi| + |\partial_y p_\varphi| \,\bigr\rangle .
\end{equation}
By sweeping $\varphi$ we obtain the curve in \cref{fig:interp-confound}. It is lossless at multiples of $90^\circ$ and features ~21\% loss at every other angle.

There is one more trap: the blur is oriented. Bilinear resampling is convolution with the separable kernel of \cref{eq:bilinear}. It's effective point-spread function is anisotropic with an orientation that tracks $\varphi$. Therefore:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:magnitude-not-sufficient}
  E_{\text{ctrl}}(\varphi) = \text{const}
  \;\;\not\Longrightarrow\;\;
  \mathrm{PSF}_{\text{ctrl}}(\varphi) = \text{const} .
\end{equation}
In words: flattening the magnitude cue is necessary but not sufficient.

 This is what will rank the four controls below. The controls are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Matched blur: solve for a per-angle isotropic $\sigma(\varphi)$ that flattens \cref{eq:sharpness} to its worst case:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matched-blur}
  \sigma(\varphi) \;:\; E\bigl(G_{\sigma(\varphi)} * p_\varphi\bigr) \;=\; \min_{\varphi'} E(\varphi')
  \quad \forall \varphi .
\end{equation}
It only addresses the magnitude cue.
\item Randomised blur: Draw the blur independently of the angle
\begin{equation}\label{eq:randomised-blur}
  \sigma \sim \mathcal{U}(0, \sigma_{\max}), \quad \sigma_{\max} = 0.8,
  \qquad p \;\leftarrow\; G_\sigma * p_\varphi .
\end{equation}
It has one limitation: randomisation adds angle-independent variance but leaves the systematic term intact:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:randomised-limit}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[E \mid \varphi\bigr] \;\text{still dips at } 45^\circ .
\end{equation}
\item Supersampling: bicubically upsample the source by $k$ before reading \cref{eq:inverse-warp}. This way the fractional offsets of \cref{eq:bilinear} shrink
 \begin{equation}\label{eq:supersample}
  \delta^{(k)} \;=\; O(1/k), \qquad k = 4 ,
\end{equation}
This makes resampling near-lossles and its PSF near angle-independent. Both cues are removed - \emph{this is the control that was selected and all the results rest on}. \Cref{fig:ss-patches} shows the effect on a single patch. Note that for $k=4$ the residual sharpness ratio is 0.96 versus 0.81 for the raw path - we removed $\approx96\%$ of the signature, not $100\%$.
\item Dominant isotropic low-pass: swamp the anisotropy with one heavy isotropic blur for each path:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dominant-lp}
  \mathrm{PSF}_{\text{eff}}(\varphi) \;=\; \mathrm{PSF}_{\text{interp}}(\varphi) * G_{\sigma_{\mathrm{lp}}},
  \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{lp}} = 1.0 ,
\end{equation}
This will result in the heaviest signal loss of the four.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The relative head}\label{sec:relative-head}
The encoder takes the input pseudo-image and turns it into $N$ patch tokens $X'$. The relative head maps and ordered pair $9i,j)$ to $\hat\theta{ij}$ - it is the prediction against the target \cref{eq:target-ropart}. A standard Vision Transformer encoder is used, with one exception:
\begin{align}
  X &= \mathrm{PatchEmbed}(\tilde I) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d},
  &
  Z^{(0)} &= \bigl[\,X \,;\, x_{\mathrm{cls}}\,\bigr] \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+1) \times d},
  \label{eq:vit-embed}\\
  \tilde Z^{(\ell)} &= Z^{(\ell-1)} + \mathrm{MHSA}\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(Z^{(\ell-1)})\bigr),
  &
  Z^{(\ell)} &= \tilde Z^{(\ell)} + \mathrm{MLP}\bigl(\mathrm{LN}(\tilde Z^{(\ell)})\bigr),
  \label{eq:vit-block}\\
  X' &= \mathrm{LN}\bigl(Z^{(L)}\bigr)_{1:N} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}.
  &&
  \label{eq:vit-out}
\end{align}
That exception is that no position embeddings are passed. $E_{\mathrm{pos}}$ does exist as a parameter but is not passed until the classification finetuning. This is the same premise as in PART ~\cite{part}. Because attention is permutation invariant and \cref{eq:retile} contains no geometry, the encoder sees an unordered set - geometry is the supervision.

Pair sampling is a crucial step in the process. $M$ ordered pairs are drawn (without replacement) from the $N^2$ possibilities. This step happens once per forward pass and is shared across the batch:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pair-sampling}
  \mathrm{idx} \subset \{0, \dots, N^2 - 1\},\; |\mathrm{idx}| = M,
  \qquad
  i = \bigl\lfloor \mathrm{idx} / N \bigr\rfloor,
  \quad
  j = \mathrm{idx} \bmod N .
\end{equation}
At $P=32,N=49$ and $M=2048$, $M/N^2\approx85\%$ of all ordered pairs (including $N$ diagonal cells) are sampled. $2048$ was chosen as upstream swept $512-4096$.

The next step is to construct the query. There are two options
\begin{itemize}
\item $\texttt{patch\_cat}$: builds the query from the two patch features, no positional code enters the head at any point
\item $\texttt{positional}$: injects a fixed sinusoidal code for the pair index into query and keys
\end{itemize}
So for pair $m=(i,j)$ the options are
\begin{align}
  q_m^{\texttt{patch\_cat}} &= W_{\mathrm{in}}\bigl[\,x'_i \,;\, x'_j\,\bigr] + b_{\mathrm{in}},
  \qquad W_{\mathrm{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 2d},
  \label{eq:query-patchcat}\\
  q_m^{\texttt{positional}} &= \mathrm{PE}_{2\mathrm{d}}[i, j, :],
  \qquad\text{with keys } x'_n \leftarrow x'_n + \mathrm{PE}_{1\mathrm{d}}[n].
  \label{eq:query-positional}
\end{align}
During experiments on a single batch, \texttt{patch\_cat} was able to overfit and learn normally whereas \texttt{positional} collapsed to the mean-prediction floor. Those experiments were ran on the baseline model and resulted in the decision to opt to \texttt{patch\_cat}. Note that this is limited to this configuration and by no mean states a general rule.

Following the query, we construct a single cross-attention layer with a single head. The queries are $Q = [q_1; \dots; q_M]$ and keys/values the patch tokens are $X'$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cross-attention}
  Z \;=\; \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{(Q W_Q)(X' W_K)^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)(X' W_V)\, W_O .
\end{equation}
Note that PART~\cite{part} writes $\operatorname{softmax}(\hat X X'^{\!\top}/\sqrt{d})\,X'$, omitting the four internal
projections $W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O$ that \py{nn.MultiheadAttention} applies. The form above in \cref{eq:cross-attention} is more accurate than the schematic in the paper.

Finally, we construct a final linear map to the channel width of \cref{eq:num-channels}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:head-out}
  \hat\theta \;=\; Z\,W_{\mathrm{out}} + b_{\mathrm{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times C},
  \qquad W_{\mathrm{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times C}.
\end{equation}
Note that $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the only tensor whose shape depends on the build order.

\section{Experimental infrastructure}\label{sec:infrastructure}
The scripts were carefully designed to avoid wasting GPU time on failed runs. To achieve this, auto-resume functionality was introduced. Training scripts automatically check for \py{\{OUT\}/checkpoint.pth} and on a hit, resume from that checkpoint. There is however, a worse issue than a missing checkpoint - a truncated checkpoint may not crash the script but will nevertheless corrupt the results. To avoid this issue, atomic checkpointing was introduced. While the checkpoint is overwritten every epoch, we added functionality to save snapshots of the model every $N$ epochs. The runs were tracked semi-online using Weights and Biases in offline mode and a custom script to sync periodically.

In terms of optimisation, AdamW with $\beta = (0.9, 0.999)$ and weight decay $0.05$ applied to matrices only (excluding biases, norms, \py{cls\_token} and \py{pos\_embed}) was used. The following per-epoch schedule was used:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lr-schedule}
  \eta(e) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \eta_{\mathrm{warm}} + (\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\mathrm{warm}})\dfrac{e}{E_w},
      & e < E_w, \\[10pt]
    \eta_{\min} + (\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})\,\dfrac{1}{2}\!\left(1 + \cos \pi \dfrac{e - E_w}{E - E_w}\right),
      & e \geq E_w,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
It is a linear warmup into cosine decay. Parameter values: $\eta_{\max} = 5\!\times\!10^{-4}$, $\eta_{\mathrm{warm}} = 10^{-6}$,
$\eta_{\min} = 10^{-5}$, $E_w = 5$, $E = 250$, batch size $192$. Note the difference mentioned in \cref{tab:upstream-divergences} - the scaling no longer silently changes $\eta$ with batch size.

Before diving in any further, a clear distinction has to be made. Evaluation of the models is dense - all four gates are computed on the full $N\times N$ ordered-pair table. Training is sampled: $M=2048$ pairs are selected for training. As a consequence, the gates are not measured on the training subsample.

If we were to quote results in raw pixel RMSE we would have no scale and would not be able to spot a collapsed head. In this project, the evidence will be presented as distance from the mean-predictor null. The metric to reflect that will be translation/rotation floor. Every result will be quoted as a fraction of a floor. The translation floor is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trans-floor}
  \operatorname{Var}(\Delta x)
  \;=\; \frac{2\operatorname{Var}(c)}{P^2}
  \;=\; \frac{M_{\text{pos}}^{2} - 1}{6 P^{2}} .
\end{equation}
Where patch centres are $c=x_s+P/2$ with the corner $x_s$ i.i.d. uniform over $M_{\text{pos}}$ integer offsets (\cref{eq:box-sampling}), so with the centred convention of \cref{sec:position-convention}, $\Delta x = (c_j - c_i)/P$. Note that the floor is $6.06$ for the baseline ($m=0$) and $5.21$ for the $\pm30^\circ$ ($m=7$). Note that this shows that the margin reduced the target distribution. One could perform a common target spread normalisation to put both on one scale by multiplying the raw error by the ratio of target standard deviations. We accumulate \texttt{floor\_x} empirically and find that the floors calculated above match the empirical results to $0.1\%$. 

Similarly, we report rotation floor. For a full circle, predicting $(\hat c, \hat s)=(0,0)$ gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rot-floor-uniform}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl[\cos^2\Delta\varphi + \sin^2\Delta\varphi\bigr] \;=\; 1 \quad\text{exactly},
\end{equation}
This was calculated in anticipation of models with full unrestricted rotation. For bounded rotation, $\varphi \sim \mathcal{U}[-a, a]$ i.i.d.,
$\mathbb{E}[\cos\varphi] = \sin a / a$ and $\mathbb{E}[\sin\varphi] = 0$, so $\mathbb{E}[\cos\Delta\varphi] = (\sin a / a)^2$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rot-floor-bounded}
  \operatorname{Var}(\cos\Delta\varphi) + \operatorname{Var}(\sin\Delta\varphi)
  \;=\; 1 - \Bigl(\frac{\sin a}{a}\Bigr)^{\!4}
  \;=\;
  \begin{cases} 0.169, & a = 30^\circ,\\ 0.836, & a = 90^\circ.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
During training, \texttt{rot\_floor=1.0} was mistakenly hard-coded for all training runs, resulting in overly conservative estimates. This had no impact on training and the correct numbers can be obtained via a simple calculation. For reporting, a different metric of the rotation channel was used - angular floor. $\Delta\varphi = \varphi_j - \varphi_i$ with $\varphi \sim
\mathcal{U}[-a,a]$ is triangular on $[-2a, 2a]$ with density
$f(t) = (2a - |t|)/(4a^2)$, so the constant predictor $\Delta\varphi = 0$ - which is
the circular median - achieves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ang-floor}
  \mathbb{E}\bigl|\Delta\varphi\bigr| \;=\; \tfrac{2}{3} a
  \;=\;
  \begin{cases} 20.0^\circ, & a = 30^\circ,\\ 60.0^\circ, & a = 90^\circ,\end{cases}
  \qquad\text{and } 90^\circ \text{ for the full circle.}
\end{equation}
Note that this definition permits $\pm90^\circ$ worse absolute angular error while learning orientation better once floor-normalised. The measured values were $19.96^\circ$ and $59.89^\circ$ - any rotation results will be read agains either of the two numbers.

Angular error itself is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-ang-mae}
  \mathrm{MAE}_\varphi
  = \Bigl\langle\, \bigl|\operatorname{wrap}\bigl(\operatorname{atan2}(\hat s_{ij}, \hat c_{ij}) - \Delta\varphi_{ij}\bigr)\bigr| \,\Bigr\rangle_{i \neq j},
\end{equation}
and is the headline rotation metric.

Finally, the models predictions must pass through four intrinsic gates. Denote $\hat t_{ij} = (\hat\theta^x_{ij}, \hat\theta^y_{ij})$ and
$\hat u_{ij} = (\hat c_{ij}, \hat s_{ij})$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Floor ratios and pixel error:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-floor-ratio}
  \frac{\operatorname{MSE}_x}{\operatorname{Var}(\Delta x)},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{px}} \;=\; P\sqrt{\bigl\langle\, \lVert \hat t_{ij} - t_{ij} \rVert^2 \,\bigr\rangle_{i \neq j}} .
\end{equation}
This helps us figure out if anything is being learned at all - without the floor a collapsed mean-predictor could still post low loss. This is a necessary condition but not sufficient
\item Identity, query the head on $(i,i)$ where the answer is known (\cref{eq:inv-identity}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-identity}
  \mathrm{Id}_{xy} = P\sqrt{\bigl\langle \lVert \hat t_{ii} \rVert^2 \bigr\rangle_i},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Id}_{\mathrm{rot}} = \sqrt{\bigl\langle (\hat c_{ii} - 1)^2 + \hat s_{ii}^2 \bigr\rangle_i} .
\end{equation}
This guards against the model producing an image-level average (which can beat the floor) rather than reading the two queried slots. 
\item Negative symmetry
\begin{align}
  \rho^{\mathrm{sym}}_{xy}
    &= \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i \neq j} \lVert \hat t_{ij} + \hat t_{ji} \rVert^2}
                  {\sum_{i \neq j} \lVert \hat t_{ij} \rVert^2}},
  \label{eq:metric-negsym-xy}\\
  \rho^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\mathrm{rot}}
    &= \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i \neq j} \bigl[(\hat c_{ij} - \hat c_{ji})^2 + (\hat s_{ij} + \hat s_{ji})^2\bigr]}
                  {\sum_{i \neq j} \bigl[\hat c_{ij}^2 + \hat s_{ij}^2\bigr]}} .
  \label{eq:metric-negsym-rot}
\end{align}
It is a ratio of the residual and the signal - that makes it scale-free. For a perfectly consistent head, both $\to 0$. \Cref{eq:metric-negsym-rot} tests the cosine-even/sine-odd pair of \cref{eq:inv-negsym} simultaneously.
\item Compositional consistency, over random distinct triples $(i,j,k)$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-comp-xy}
  \rho^{\mathrm{comp}}_{xy}
  = \sqrt{\frac{\sum \lVert \hat t_{ik} - (\hat t_{ij} + \hat t_{jk}) \rVert^2}
               {\sum \lVert \hat t_{ik} \rVert^2}},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-comp-rot}
  \mathrm{MAE}^{\mathrm{comp}}_{\mathrm{rot}}
  = \Bigl\langle\; \bigl|\operatorname{wrap}\bigl(\widehat{\Delta\varphi}_{ik} - \widehat{\Delta\varphi}_{ij} - \widehat{\Delta\varphi}_{jk}\bigr)\bigr| \;\Bigr\rangle,
  \qquad \widehat{\Delta\varphi} = \operatorname{atan2}(\hat s, \hat c).
\end{equation}
Note \cref{eq:metric-comp-rot} composes the \emph{decoded angles}, not the
$(\cos,\sin)$ vectors, so it is insensitive to the head's output norm.
\end{enumerate}

We can combine all the criteria into a single positive criterion. This is the standard by which the models will be judged in \cref{ch:pretraining}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:positive-criterion}
  \underbrace{\mathrm{MAE}_\varphi \ll \tfrac{2}{3}a}_{\text{beats the floor}}
  \;\;\wedge\;\;
  \underbrace{\mathrm{Id}_{\mathrm{rot}},\, \rho^{\mathrm{sym}}_{\mathrm{rot}},\, \mathrm{MAE}^{\mathrm{comp}}_{\mathrm{rot}} \to 0}_{\text{invariants hold}}
  \;\;\wedge\;\;
  \underbrace{\text{survives control (c)}}_{\text{not the signature}} .
\end{equation}
